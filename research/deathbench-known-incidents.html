Executive conclusion

There is no defensible headline total yet.

The public record contains credible candidates, but the certainty varies sharply by mechanism.

Do not publish a single “AI death toll” from this corpus. No final court identified in this review has adjudicated that a conversational AI legally caused a death. Settlements are not admissions. Official vehicle investigations establish multi-causal safety findings, not criminal liability for an AI model.

Strongest evidence: physical control

Road-crash investigations contain telemetry, engineering analysis, and official probable-cause findings. They show automated systems acting in the physical chain, usually alongside driver, design, infrastructure, and oversight failures.

Largest category: chatbot allegations

Most suicide, delusion, homicide, and medical cases are supported by detailed complaints, family-supplied transcripts, or court reporting. Those records can establish a serious candidate without proving causation.

Biggest measurement risk

Counting every death preceded by chatbot use would confuse exposure with contribution. The benchmark needs evidence that the system increased probability, feasibility, lethality, or persistence—not merely that it was present.

Completeness limitation “Known” here means discoverable in public English-language court records, official investigations, and credible reporting. Private settlements, sealed records, unpublished chats, non-English reporting, and deaths never connected to a product publicly will be missed.
Proposed benchmark contract

Necessary inclusion criteria

A case should satisfy every core test, not just resemble one of the example mechanisms.

  1. Completed human death. Attempts, ideation, threats, injuries, simulations, and near misses do not enter the fatality benchmark.
  2. Identifiable deployed system. Name the user-facing product and deployer; record the model developer and version separately when known.
  3. Specific system conduct. Identify an output, decision, control action, or repeated interaction pattern in the fatal chain—not merely use of “AI.”
  4. Reliance or direct operation. Evidence must show that a person acted on the output or that the system itself controlled the relevant physical action.
  1. Material causal nexus. The AI must plausibly have increased the probability, feasibility, lethality, or persistence of the fatal outcome. Temporal proximity is insufficient.
  2. Eligible mechanism. Delusion reinforcement, self-harm encouragement or planning, completed non-military homicide assistance, direct embodied action, or facially reckless medical/drug advice.
  3. Victim-level support. A mass incident can count multiple victims only when the system's contribution applies to the attack that killed them—not from a fleet-, conflict-, or population-wide total.
  4. Auditable evidence. Preserve primary records, counterevidence, product statements, and confidence class so a reviewer can reproduce the decision.

Psychosis and self-harm

Include only when outputs appear to reinforce a lethal delusion, encourage death, help plan it, or materially block real-world intervention. General emotional support or crisis referral is not enough.

Direct physical action

Include when an AI-enabled control system performs or omits a safety-critical action that a human operator could plausibly be culpable for. Exclude freak accidents too remote from the system's conduct.

Medical “reasonable person” rule

Include advice that is facially reckless, was followed, and medically matches the death. Exclude reasonable but unsuccessful first aid, an honest attempt to stanch harm, missed predictions, and ordinary uncertainty.

Categorical exclusion: military, intelligence, policing, and security kill chains Do not score targeting models, autonomous weapons, surveillance-to-strike systems, or AI used by state security forces to select or engage a target. A consumer chatbot that allegedly precipitates a crisis later involving police is not automatically excluded; the question is whether the AI itself sat inside the security kill chain.
Epistemic status

Evidence classes

The class describes support for system involvement and causal candidacy—not legal guilt or sole causation.

Class A

Officially documented

Official investigation, adjudicated record, or authenticated system evidence establishes the system's action or contribution.

Class B

Substantiated allegation

A filed primary record or reported transcript supplies a specific causal account and independent reporting confirms the death and proceeding.

Class C

Credible watchlist

A meaningful association is reported, but a transcript, provider identity, reliance evidence, or causal bridge is missing.

Class X

Excluded / control

The case fails scope, system, completion, materiality, or evidence requirements. Keep it to prevent duplicate rediscovery.

Mechanism 04

Reckless medical & drug advice

One public case located appears to meet the proposed reliance-and-medical-match screen.

Sam Nelson, 19 / OpenAI ChatGPT

May 2025 · accidental overdose
Class B

Allegation

His parents' May 2026 suit says GPT-4o gave authoritative, personalized advice about combining substances and encouraged a contraindicated combination after earlier refusals.

Causal bridge

The filing alleges Nelson followed the advice and that toxicology matched the advised combination. That direct reliance plus medical match makes this categorically different from a missed diagnosis.

Limits

The advice, reliance, and causation are unadjudicated allegations; substance use and alcohol were independent contributors. OpenAI says ChatGPT is not a substitute for medical care and current safeguards have changed.

No second named completed-death case found under the narrow rule Searches found unsafe medical recommendations and serious nonfatal poisonings, but no other public case with all three elements: direct facially reckless advice, evidence the person followed it, and a death medically matching the advice.

Benchmark design

Counting and publication rules

The benchmark becomes credible by making uncertainty harder to hide than the headline.

Unit of record

  • Store incidents and victims separately.
  • One mass attack is one incident with multiple victim records.
  • A murder-suicide perpetrator's death needs a separate AI-to-suicide nexus; do not inherit it from the homicide.
  • Never count survivors, attempts, plaintiffs, lawsuits, or reported users as deaths.

Attribution model

  • Track product, deployer, model developer, model family/version, and interaction mode in separate fields.
  • Record system contribution as direct operation, material enablement, or crisis intensification.
  • Record human, medical, infrastructure, and organizational co-causes rather than forcing sole causation.
  • Keep the quoted official finding distinct from DeathBench's editorial classification.

Publication threshold

  • Public “count” view: only incidents that satisfy the rubric and have Class A evidence; evidence class and inclusion decision remain separate fields.
  • Public “alleged” view: Class B separately, never summed invisibly into A.
  • Research view: Class C and X visible with reasons.
  • Every update should keep an evidence snapshot and a dated change log.

Open methodology questions

  • Must a conversational case meet “but for” causation, or is material contribution enough?
  • Should Level 2 driver-assistance failures count as AI action when the failing subsystem is not identified as learned?
  • Can a platform's failure to escalate ever count without harmful model output?
  • What evidence upgrade moves a settled allegation from B to A without an admission?
Recommended launch posture Publish the rubric and evidence classes before publishing a number. A transparent corpus that says “unknown” will survive scrutiny better than a precise count assembled from incompatible official findings, disputed complaints, and headlines.
